Offering 01 · Lead topic · Human ↔ machine

What Must Stay Human

Creativity, judgment, and ownership in the age of AI. Most AI conversations ask what the technology can do. This one asks what leaders must deliberately keep human, and how to tell whether they have.

01

The premise

AI is arriving in every workplace faster than the norms for using it. Leaders are told what the tools can do. They are rarely helped to decide which parts of the work hold their organization's judgment and creativity, or how to protect those parts while still using powerful tools.

Healthcare is a sharp test case. Errors are measured in harm, and accountability can't be handed to a machine. John trains physicians to innovate with AI as a partner rather than a substitute. This session translates those practices for any organization.

The core proposition

Perception, judgment, and decision stay with the person. Ownership is shown, not policed.

Drawn from the AI covenant in the Physician Innovator Track: “where perception, judgment, and decision must remain with the learner, and how the ledger proves ownership rather than policing use.”

02

What participants experience

The workshop is built like a simulation: do the work, then debrief it. The keynote version brings the same arc to the stage with live audience moments.

Observe before solving

A short field exercise in noticing, with no solutions allowed. It trains the perception that AI can't supply.

Work the problem with AI

Teams tackle a realistic challenge with AI tools available, and keep a simple ledger of what the tool contributed.

Debrief what stayed human

A structured, simulation-style debrief. Where did judgment live? What did the team notice that the tool missed? Where did someone change their mind?

Test the tool, not just the output

Leaders practice the questions that matter: does it stay accurate over time, and is it fair to everyone it touches?

Plan the rehearsal

Treat an AI rollout like a new device in a resuscitation room. Rehearse it where the work happens, before anyone depends on it.

Write the covenant

Each team drafts a short AI covenant for its own unit: what stays human, and how ownership will be visible.

03

Participants will

  1. Distinguish work AI can accelerate from the perception, judgment, and decisions that must remain with people.
  2. Use two lightweight practices, an AI ledger and a team AI covenant, that make human ownership visible without banning tools.
  3. See why observing before solving, and recording reversals and false starts, protects creative capacity.
  4. Ask sharper questions of any AI tool about consistency over time and equity across the people it serves.
  5. Leave with a plan to rehearse an AI rollout before go-live.

05

Signature stories

Each story is drawn from John's own work and carries one lesson executives can take back.

Rehearse first

Introducing a new technology into rural emergency departments

In an AHRQ-funded study, John and colleagues used in-situ simulation to bring new technology for sepsis care into rural emergency departments. They rehearsed it where the work happens, with the people who would use it. The approach became a published framework, the 3-Act-3-Debrief model.

Lesson: an AI rollout is a new device in the room. Rehearse it before anyone depends on it.

Advances in Simulation 2020; Journal of Patient Safety 2022

Name the unknown

Teaching doctors to say “I'm not sure,” well

John helped design a mobile game and a mastery-learning program that teach physicians to communicate diagnostic uncertainty to patients at discharge. The program was tested in a randomized trial.

Lesson: uncertainty is a human skill. As tools sound more confident, people must get better at naming what isn't known.

Patient Education and Counseling 2022; Academic Medicine 2023

Test the tool

Does the model stay true, for everyone?

John co-authored research asking whether a mortality-prediction model performs consistently across time and equitably across populations. It's the question every leader should put to any AI tool.

Lesson: leaders don't have to build models. They do have to ask whether a model stays accurate over time and fair to everyone it touches.

Journal of Medical Systems 2023

Prove ownership

The ledger and the covenant

In the Physician Innovator Track John designed, each resident logs one AI ledger row per working session. The cohort agrees to its AI covenant out loud. The final review asks what AI contributed, and what judgment stayed yours.

Lesson: you don't have to ban the tool to keep the judgment. Make ownership visible.

Physician Innovator Track, University of Illinois College of Medicine Peoria (opens July 2027) · drvoz.github.io/physician_innovator

Not new, newly urgent

Twenty years of machine-assisted judgment

John's published work on computerized order entry and online decision support dates to 2004. The question of how clinicians and machines share a decision isn't new. The scale and the stakes are.

Lesson: the organizations that handled decision support well are a guide to handling AI well.

Academic Emergency Medicine 2004
