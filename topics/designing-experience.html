Offering 02 · Knowing ↔ doing

Designing Experience That Changes Behavior

Organizations spend fortunes telling capable people what to do, then wonder why behavior doesn't change. Simulation was built for the opposite problem: making people competent at what is too rare, risky, or complex to learn on the job.

01

The premise

Experience is a poor teacher on its own. The events that matter most are rare. Feedback comes late or not at all, and there is seldom time to reflect. In 2005 John gave a talk titled “How Simulation Can Succeed Where Experiential Learning Fails.” Two decades of building simulations, serious games, and immersive curricula have only sharpened that argument.

The principles that let a resuscitation team rehearse a crisis carry over to any organization trying to change behavior: a sales floor, a leadership team, a plant, a newly merged company.

02

Six design principles

The framework at the center of the session. Participants apply each principle to a behavior their own organization needs to change.

Start from the behavior

Design backward from what people must do differently, not from the content you want to cover.

Make it real enough to matter

Fidelity is emotional and cognitive, not just technical. Well-designed simulations evoke real stress responses, and participants ask for more.

Rehearse where the work happens

In-situ practice in the real workplace surfaces the friction and hazards a classroom never shows.

The debrief is the lesson

Structured reflection turns an experience into a changed behavior. It is also one of the most underused leadership tools.

Make it safe to be honest

People only reflect truthfully in a safe learning climate, and that climate can be designed.

Measure the change

Mastery standards, observed performance, and economic impact show whether the experience worked.

03

Signature stories

Standard-setting

Simulation for every medical specialty

As chair of the American Board of Medical Specialties Simulation Planning Group, John helped plan how simulation would be used to maintain board certification across all medical specialties.

Lesson: designed experience can become how an entire profession proves competence, not just a training extra.

Behavioral engineering

Engineering a safe learning climate

John designed and taught an immersive behavior-change program for residents and faculty, about 60 learners a year from 2014 to 2019. Its aim was to improve the learning climate in clinical training.

Lesson: culture is a set of behaviors, and behaviors can be rehearsed.

Serious games

A game that teaches a conversation

A mobile game, co-designed with colleagues, teaches residents how to talk with patients about diagnostic uncertainty. John's immersive work also includes a virtual-reality intubation trainer, interactive mixed reality for sepsis education, and a virtual-world oral examination.

Lesson: choose the medium for the behavior. A phone game can change a conversation better than a lecture.

Design on a budget

Low-cost simulators, patented designs

John has published designs for inexpensive simulators, including models for cricothyrotomy, epistaxis, and melanoma screening on simulated back skin, and holds a U.S. simulator patent, with two further patent applications.

Lesson: realism comes from what the learner must do, not what the equipment costs.

Efficiency

Can learners debrief themselves?

John and colleagues tested whether video self-review could match faculty-led debriefing after simulation, a direct question about where expert time is best spent. Separate work measured the economic impact of simulation-based training.

Lesson: treat learning design like any other investment. Test it, and redeploy scarce experts where they matter most.

04

Participants will

  1. Apply six design principles to a behavior their organization needs to change.
  2. Use debriefing as a leadership tool, not only a training technique.
  3. Judge when immersive technology (VR, mixed reality, games) earns its cost, and when a simple model will do.
  4. Build the psychological safety that honest reflection requires.
  5. Choose measures that show whether behavior actually changed.
