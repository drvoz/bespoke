Making it safe to practice the moments that matter

Three thinkers frame the work. K. Anders Ericsson explained how expertise is built. Gary Klein showed how experts actually decide. Robert Wears asked how systems stay safe when work never goes as planned. Each thread below is tagged to the lens it carries.

The people who shaped how I see safety

Each lens is a different answer to the same question: why do capable clinicians, in good systems, still hurt patients, and what can rehearsal do about it?

Expertise · deliberate practice

K. Anders Ericsson

1947–2020 · Florida State University

Expert performance is built, not born: focused practice at the edge of ability, with immediate feedback and repetition. Experience alone does not make an expert.

Documented connection

Presented his overview of deliberate practice at the 2008 Academic Emergency Medicine Consensus Conference that John co-chaired. Ericsson KA. Deliberate practice and acquisition of expert performance: a general overview. Acad Emerg Med. 2008;15(11):988–994.

In the work: procedural competency labs, mastery learning for paracentesis (2012), and the mastery-learning trial for communicating diagnostic uncertainty (2023).

Decisions · naturalistic decision making

Gary Klein

Recognition-primed decision model · the premortem

Experts under time pressure rarely compare options. They recognize the situation, then mentally simulate one course of action. Imagining the failure in advance, a premortem, surfaces risks that planning misses.

Documented connection

Direct conversations between John and Klein led to a principle for teaching: help learners recognize patterns, and expertise follows. Klein discusses it in Streetlights and Shadows: Searching for the Keys to Adaptive Decision Making. MIT Press; 2009.

In the work: simulation as a pattern library for events too rare to learn on shift; failure mode analysis of sepsis resuscitation (2014); debriefing as prospective hazard analysis (2024); the Oli postpartum hemorrhage simulation.

Systems · resilience engineering

Robert L. Wears

1947–2017 · University of Florida, Jacksonville

Emergency physician and safety scientist. Safety comes from how people adapt as work-as-done departs from work-as-imagined, so study how things go right as well as how they fail. Co-editor of Resilient Health Care; co-author of Still Not Safe.

Documented connection

Co-authors on the national handoffs consensus paper. Cheung D, Kelley J, Beach C, et al, Vozenilek J, Wears R, White M. Improving handoffs in the emergency department. Ann Emerg Med. 2010;55(2):171–180.

In the work: handoff research, in-situ simulation in the real rooms where care happens, and rehearsing new technology before go-live.

Oli: will an early warning change what a team does?

Case in focus · August 2024

Baymatob, an Australian maternal health company, brought Oli PPH to Jump to test its workflow fit and health-economic value before its U.S. launch. Oli is a wearable labor monitor with FDA Breakthrough Device Designation that flags women at risk of postpartum hemorrhage, the leading cause of preventable maternal death. John designed and led the study with Dr. Lisa Barker. The question was not whether nurses could use the device. It was whether its alert would change how a real team prepares, decides, and acts.

Design

Each team first applied the device, then worked five scenarios that crossed the alert with the outcome, so teams met true alerts, missed hemorrhages, and false alarms:

What the teams showed us

  • Readiness moved earlier. An alert prompted hemorrhage medications at the bedside, a second IV, and blood on hold before any bleeding.
  • The algorithm did not move. Once a hemorrhage began, teams ran the same protocol whether or not Oli had predicted it.
  • False alarms were acceptable; misses were not. Clinicians preferred a false positive to a false negative, and trust would hinge on evidence of accuracy.
  • Skill erosion was a real worry. Senior nurses feared newer nurses could come to depend on the alert.
  • The display had to fit the system. A bedside alert sat outside the central monitoring teams already use, and an ambiguous grey state looked like a frozen screen.

Expertise The skill-erosion concern is Ericsson's warning in reverse: take away the practice and the expertise fades.

Decisions The alert worked as a premortem and a recognition cue. It changed preparation, not the decision rules teams already trusted.

Systems Findings were organized as resiliencies and vulnerabilities for each value proposition, judging the device by how it fits work-as-done.

Source: Jump Simulation, Baymatob Oli: Estimating Readiness for Change in PPH Monitoring Workflows, report to Baymatob, September 2024; OSF Innovation case study.

The through-line

“Rehearse it where the work happens, with the people who will do it, before anyone depends on it.”

Ericsson supplied the method: deliberate, feedback-rich practice. Klein, in conversation, sharpened what that practice should build: recognition of patterns, fast enough to act on. Wears widened the frame from the clinician to the system, where safety is something people make every shift.

The through-line is rehearsal. A sepsis bundle, a handoff, a new building, a telehealth cart, and an AI tool all get the same treatment: practice them in place first, then debrief what the practice revealed. That is the foundation under every offering on this site.
